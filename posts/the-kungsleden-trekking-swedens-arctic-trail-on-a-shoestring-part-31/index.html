The Kungsleden: Trekking Sweden's Arctic Trail on a Shoestring - Part 31

2026-07-21 · Treks · Tags: The, treks, backpackerguide

When most backpackers dream of Scandinavia, they picture fjords, expensive beer, and a wallet crying for mercy. But tucked inside the Arctic Circle lies one of Europe's most spectacular and surprisingly affordable long-distance treks: the Kungsleden, or the King's Trail. Stretching 440 kilometers between Abisko and Hemavan, this legendary route cuts through birch forests, across frozen plateaus, and past some of Sweden's most dramatic peaks. The best part? You don't need a guide, a permit, or a fortune to walk it. With a bit of planning and a willingness to embrace the simple life, the Kungsleden can be done on a shoestring budget without sacrificing the adventure of a lifetime. Here's how to make it happen.

Why the Kungsleden?

The Kungsleden is not just a trail; it's a rite of passage for European trekkers. It offers a unique blend of Arctic wilderness and well-marked paths, making it accessible to beginners while still challenging for seasoned hikers. The classic section from Abisko to Nikkaluokta (about 110 kilometers) is the most popular, taking you through the stunning Abisko National Park, past the turquoise waters of Lake Tjaktjajaure, and up to the summit of Kebnekaise, Sweden's highest peak. But the trail continues south, offering even more solitude and raw beauty. What makes it perfect for budget travelers is that the Swedish Tourist Association (STF) maintains a network of mountain huts along the route, but you don't have to use them. Camping is free (with the right to roam), and the huts offer affordable dorm beds if you're a member. The trail itself is free, and there are no permit fees—just the cost of getting there and feeding yourself.

Getting There Without Breaking the Bank

The biggest expense for most trekkers is the journey to the trailhead. Abisko is the northern terminus, and it's accessible by train from Stockholm via the Iron Ore Line. The overnight train from Stockholm to Abisko takes about 18 hours, but if you book in advance, you can snag a seat in a sleeping car for as little as €50. Alternatively, you can fly to Kiruna, the nearest airport, and then take a bus or train to Abisko. Budget airlines like Norwegian and SAS often have deals from major European cities, but you'll need to factor in baggage fees for your trekking gear. A cheaper option is to take the bus from Stockholm to Kiruna, which can cost around €40 if booked early, but it's a long ride. Once you're in Abisko, the trailhead is right at the Abisko Turiststation, which is a short walk from the train station. If you're starting from the southern end, Hemavan has a small airport with connections from Stockholm, but it's often pricier. My advice: start in Abisko and walk south, as the prevailing winds are at your back, and you'll have the classic views of Kebnekaise ahead of you.

Huts vs. Camping: The Budget Decision

One of the biggest cost decisions on the Kungsleden is where you'll sleep. The STF huts are comfortable, with bunk beds, a kitchen, and sometimes a sauna. They cost around 350 SEK (about €30) per night for non-members, and 250 SEK for members. That adds up over a week. But you can also camp for free anywhere along the trail, thanks to the Swedish Right of Public Access (Allemansrätten). This law allows you to pitch a tent on any uncultivated land, as long as you stay at least 150 meters from a dwelling. The trail is dotted with designated camping spots near the huts, which often have toilets and water sources. Camping is the ultimate budget move—you'll save hundreds of euros over a week. However, you need to be prepared for Arctic weather, which can be harsh even in summer. A good three-season tent, a warm sleeping bag, and a sleeping pad are non-negotiable. If you're not up for carrying a tent, you can mix it up: camp a few nights and use the huts on the worst weather days. The huts also offer a place to dry your gear and cook a hot meal, which is a luxury after a day in the rain. My tip: join the STF (Swedish Tourist Association) before you go. Membership costs about 300 SEK (€27) and gives you discounts on hut stays, plus access to member-only cabins. If you plan to use the huts even a few nights, it pays for itself.

Food and Water: Fueling the Adventure on a Budget

Food is where you can really cut costs. The huts sell basic supplies like pasta, rice, and canned goods, but they're expensive—expect to pay double what you would in a supermarket. The smart move is to bring your own dehydrated meals, instant oats, nuts, and energy bars. You can buy these at home or in Stockholm before you head north. Plan for about 2,500-3,000 calories per day, which translates to roughly 500-600 grams of food per day. That's a lot to carry, but you can resupply at the mountain stations along the way, like at Sälka, Singi, and Kebnekaise. These stations have small shops with limited supplies, but they're pricey. A better strategy is to mail a resupply package to yourself at the Abisko Turiststation or the STF huts if you know your schedule. The STF allows you to send packages to their huts for a small fee. Alternatively, you can fish in the lakes and rivers—the Arctic char is abundant, and with a simple fishing kit, you can catch your dinner. Just make sure you have a valid fishing license, which you can buy at the tourist stations. Water is not an issue; the streams and lakes are crystal clear and safe to drink directly, so you don't need to carry a filter or purification tablets. That saves weight and money.

Gear and Budget Tips: What to Bring and What to Leave

You don't need fancy gear to tackle the Kungsleden, but you do need to be smart. The weather can change in an instant, so layering is key. A waterproof and windproof shell is essential, as is a warm mid-layer (fleece or down) and a base layer that wicks moisture. You'll also need sturdy hiking boots that are already broken in, and gaiters to keep snow and water out. A trekking pole or two will save your knees on the rocky sections. For camping, a lightweight tent (under 2 kg), a sleeping bag rated to at least -5°C (even in summer, nights can be cold), and a sleeping pad are must-haves. A small stove and a pot are useful for cooking, but you can also use the hut kitchens if you're staying there. To save money, consider renting gear in Abisko. The Abisko Turiststation rents tents, sleeping bags, and stoves at reasonable rates, which is perfect if you're flying in and don't want to pay baggage fees. Another budget tip: travel in a group. Splitting the cost of a tent, stove, and food can cut your expenses significantly. And don't forget to buy your alcohol at the Systembolaget (the state liquor store) in Stockholm or Kiruna before you hit the trail—it's much cheaper than at the huts.

Making the Most of the Trail: Side Trips and Extras

The Kungsleden is more than just a walk; it's a gateway to some of Sweden's most iconic experiences. If you have extra time and a little cash, consider a side trip to the summit of Kebnekaise. The hike from the Kebnekaise mountain station to the top is challenging but rewarding, and you can hire a guide for around 1,500 SEK (€140) if you're not confident. But you can also do it independently if you have experience with glacier travel—though it's risky. A cheaper alternative is to hike to the Lapporten, the famous U-shaped valley, which offers stunning views without the technical difficulty. Another must-do is a visit to the Abisko Sky Station, which offers aurora borealis viewing in winter, but in summer, you can enjoy the midnight sun. The station is accessible via a chairlift (about 200 SEK round trip), but you can also hike up. If you're on a tight budget, just find a high point and watch the sun dip below the horizon and rise again—it's free. And don't miss the chance to take a dip in a glacial lake (if you're brave) or enjoy a sauna at one of the huts—it's a quintessential Swedish experience.

Conclusion

The Kungsleden is proof that you don't need a fat wallet to experience true wilderness. With free camping, affordable hut options, and a trail that's free to access, it's one of the best value treks in Europe. The key is to plan ahead, pack smart, and embrace the simplicity of the Arctic. Whether you're a seasoned trekker or a first-timer, this trail will leave you with memories that last a lifetime—and a bank account that's still intact. So grab your boots, your tent, and your sense of adventure, and head north. The King's Trail is waiting.
