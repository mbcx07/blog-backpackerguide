The Jordan Trail: Trekking the Desert Kingdom on a Shoestring

2026-07-24 · Treks · Tags: treks, The, backpackerguide

When most backpackers think of Jordan, they picture the rose-red ruins of Petra, the otherworldly landscapes of Wadi Rum, or the salty float of the Dead Sea. But few realize that this small Middle Eastern kingdom is home to one of the world's most spectacular long-distance treks: the Jordan Trail. Stretching over 650 kilometers from the green hills of Um Qais in the north to the Red Sea port of Aqaba in the south, this trail weaves through ancient villages, dramatic canyons, and vast desert expanses. And the best part? It remains blissfully off the radar of mass tourism, meaning you can experience it on a budget that would make a hostel dorm look luxurious. As a backpacker who has walked every dusty kilometer, I'm here to show you how to tackle the Jordan Trail without breaking the bank—or your spirit.

Why the Jordan Trail is a Hidden Gem for Budget Trekkers

The Jordan Trail is not just a hike; it's a journey through 10,000 years of history. You'll pass by Roman ruins, Crusader castles, and Bedouin camps, all while traversing terrain that shifts from Mediterranean forests to lunar-like deserts. Unlike the crowded Inca Trail or the permit-heavy Alpine routes, the Jordan Trail is still raw and unregulated. There are no entry fees, no mandatory guides, and no booking systems for the trail itself. You can walk it independently, at your own pace, and for a fraction of the cost of other iconic treks. The trail is also well-marked with white and red stripes, and local communities are increasingly welcoming hikers, offering cheap homestays and meals. For budget backpackers, this is a goldmine—you get an authentic cultural experience without the tourist markup.

Planning Your Trek: Timing, Permits, and Budget

The best time to hike the Jordan Trail is between March and May, or September and November, when temperatures are mild. Summer is scorching, and winter can bring flash floods in the canyons. The trail is open year-round, but you'll want to avoid the extreme heat. As for permits, there are none for the trail itself, but you'll need a Jordan Pass if you plan to visit Petra and other major sites. The Jordan Pass (70 JOD for a 3-day pass) covers your visa fee and entry to over 40 attractions, making it a no-brainer for trekkers. Your daily budget can be as low as 20-30 JOD (about $28-42) if you camp, cook your own food, and occasionally splurge on a hostel or guesthouse. That includes food, water, and transport between trailheads. Compare that to the $500+ you'd spend on a guided trek elsewhere, and you'll see why this is a budget backpacker's dream.

Getting There and Getting Around: Navigating the Trailheads

Jordan is a small country, and public transport is surprisingly efficient. You can fly into Amman, then take a bus or shared taxi to the northern start at Um Qais. From there, the trail is well-connected to villages and towns where you can resupply. The trail is divided into 8 sections, each ending at a village or town with basic services. To get to trailheads, you'll often rely on local minibuses (servees) or hitchhiking, which is common and safe in Jordan. For example, from Amman, you can take a bus to Ajloun, then a short taxi to the trail. For the southern sections, you can use the Hejaz Railway or buses to Wadi Rum and Aqaba. I recommend carrying a physical map and a GPS app like Maps.me, as cell service is spotty in the desert. Also, be prepared to walk through some remote areas where you might not see another soul for days—that's part of the charm.

Accommodation and Camping on a Shoestring

One of the biggest expenses on any trek is accommodation, but the Jordan Trail offers plenty of free or cheap options. Wild camping is legal and safe in most areas, and you'll often find flat spots near Bedouin camps. The Bedouin are incredibly hospitable and will often invite you for tea or even a meal—just be respectful and offer a small gift or payment if they insist. In villages, you can stay at local guesthouses for as little as 10-15 JOD per night, which often includes a hearty dinner. Some sections have designated campsites with basic facilities, but they're not mandatory. I recommend carrying a lightweight tent and sleeping bag, as you'll want the flexibility to stop wherever the sunset looks best. If you're on a tight budget, you can also use the 'couchsurfing' style networks, but the trail community is small, so don't rely on it. Instead, embrace the simplicity of sleeping under the stars—it's free and unforgettable.

Food and Water: Fueling Your Desert Trek Without the Price Tag

Water is the most critical resource on the Jordan Trail, especially in the southern desert sections. You'll need to carry up to 4-5 liters in the dry stretches, and you'll find natural springs and taps in the north. Always treat water with a filter or purification tablets, as some sources are not potable. For food, the key is to buy local staples in the villages. You'll find fresh pita bread, hummus, falafel, and vegetables at incredibly low prices. A typical meal at a local eatery costs 2-3 JOD, and you can stock up on dried fruits, nuts, and canned goods for the trail. I also recommend carrying a small stove and a pot to cook simple meals like rice and lentils. The Bedouin will often offer you sweet tea and bread, which is a great energy boost. Just remember to pack out all your trash—leave no trace, especially in the fragile desert ecosystem.

Safety, Culture, and the Art of Slow Travel

Jordan is one of the safest countries in the Middle East, and the trail is generally secure. However, you should always check the weather forecast, especially for flash floods in canyons like Wadi Mujib. The trail is well-marked, but it's easy to get lost in the desert, so carry a GPS and a physical map. Culturally, you'll be passing through conservative rural areas, so dress modestly—cover your shoulders and knees, and women should consider a headscarf when entering villages. Learning a few Arabic phrases like 'shukran' (thank you) and 'marhaba' (hello) will go a long way. The Jordan Trail is not a race; it's a cultural immersion. Take time to sit with Bedouin families, share a meal, and listen to their stories. This is where the real value lies—not in the miles you cover, but in the connections you make. And because you're on a budget, you're forced to slow down, which is exactly what the trail is about.

Conclusion

The Jordan Trail is a masterpiece of nature and history, and it's still possible to experience it without the hefty price tag of other world-class treks. With careful planning, a flexible mindset, and a willingness to embrace the local way of life, you can walk from the green hills of the north to the red sands of the south for less than the cost of a weekend in a European city. It's a trek that rewards the adventurous and the frugal alike, offering a profound sense of accomplishment and a deep connection to a land that has welcomed travelers for millennia. So pack your lightest gear, bring an open heart, and hit the trail—the desert is waiting, and it won't cost you a fortune.
