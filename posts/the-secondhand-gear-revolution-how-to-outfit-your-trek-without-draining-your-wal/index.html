The Secondhand Gear Revolution: How to Outfit Your Trek Without Draining Your Wallet

2026-05-24 · Gear · Tags: gear, backpackerguide, The

Let’s face it: the gear list for a serious trek can look like a ransom note from the outdoor industry. A down sleeping bag, a three-season tent, a lightweight pack, a water filter, trekking poles—the retail price tags add up faster than you can say “hypothermia.” But here’s the secret that budget backpackers have known for decades: you don’t need to buy any of it new. The secondhand gear market is a treasure trove of high-quality, trail-tested equipment that can save you hundreds, if not thousands, of dollars. And in an era where sustainability matters as much as savings, buying used is not just a smart financial move—it’s an ethical one. This guide will show you exactly how to build a complete backpacking kit from secondhand sources, what to look for, what to avoid, and how to make your pre-loved gear last for years to come.

Why Secondhand Makes Sense for Backpackers

First, let’s talk about the obvious: money. A brand-new ultralight tent can set you back $400 or more. A used one from a reputable brand like MSR or Big Agnes might cost you $150–200, and often it’s been used for only a handful of trips. The same goes for sleeping bags, backpacks, and even technical clothing. The depreciation on outdoor gear is brutal—most items lose 40–60% of their value the moment they leave the store. That means you can get near-new performance at a fraction of the price.

But there’s more to it than just savings. Used gear is often already “broken in.” Hiking boots that have been worn a few times have already molded to someone else’s feet—but if you buy a pair that’s barely used, you’ll still need to break them in yourself. However, items like backpacks and tents that have been through a few trips have already proven their durability. You’re not the guinea pig. Plus, buying used reduces waste and keeps perfectly good gear out of landfills—a win for your wallet and the planet.

Finally, the secondhand market is a community. You’re often buying from fellow hikers who understand the value of gear and are willing to share tips. Many sellers are happy to tell you about the trail conditions they used the gear in, what worked, and what didn’t. That’s intel you can’t get from a retail store.

Where to Find Quality Used Gear

Now that you’re convinced, where do you start? The options are more diverse than you might think.

  • Online Marketplaces: eBay, Craigslist, Facebook Marketplace, and specialized sites like GearTrade and r/ULgeartrade on Reddit are goldmines. You can filter by brand, size, and condition, and often see detailed photos. The key is to ask questions and request additional photos if needed.
  • Gear Swaps and Consignment Stores: Many outdoor retailers host annual gear swaps, especially in the spring and fall. REI’s used gear section (online and in some stores) is fantastic—they thoroughly inspect and grade items. Local consignment shops like Play It Again Sports or dedicated outdoor consignment stores are also worth checking.
  • Thrift Stores and Goodwill: It’s hit-or-miss, but you can occasionally find gems like a pristine down jacket or a vintage pack that’s built like a tank. Go often and keep an open mind.
  • Hostel Bulletin Boards and Trail Town Shops: If you’re already on the road, check the community boards in hostels, laundromats, and outdoor gear shops near trailheads. Hikers often sell their gear at the end of a long trek to lighten their load and make some cash. This is how I scored a barely-used Jetboil for $30 on the Appalachian Trail.
  • Online Forums and Social Media Groups: Facebook groups like “Backpacking Gear Swap” and “Ultralight Backpacking Gear Exchange” are active communities where gear changes hands daily. You can also find local hiking clubs that have classifieds.

When you find a promising listing, don’t just look at the price. Check the seller’s reputation, read the description carefully, and ask for specifics about any wear or damage. A good seller will be upfront about issues.

What to Look For (and What to Avoid) When Buying Used Gear

Not all secondhand gear is a steal. Some items are better bought new, and some used items are ticking time bombs. Here’s how to separate the diamonds from the rough.

Items that are safe to buy used:

  • Backpacks: Look for broken zippers, worn hip belts, and frayed straps. Check the seams for any tears or delamination. A pack that’s structurally sound can last for decades.
  • Tents: Inspect the fly for UV damage (look for discoloration or brittleness), check the poles for cracks or bends, and examine the floor for punctures or worn waterproofing. Ask if the tent has ever been used in high winds or heavy rain—that’s where hidden damage occurs.
  • Sleeping bags: Check the loft (fluffiness) of the insulation. If down, make sure it’s not clumped or smelly. For synthetic, check for compression damage—if it doesn’t spring back, it’s lost its warmth. Also check the zipper and the shell fabric for tears.
  • Stoves and cookware: Simple canister stoves are almost indestructible. Test the ignition if possible. For liquid fuel stoves, check for leaks and worn O-rings. Pots and pans are fine as long as they’re not warped.
  • Clothing and footwear: Look for excessive wear on soles, tears in waterproof membranes, and broken zippers. For boots, check the midsole for crumbling (hydrolysis) and the tread for remaining depth. If the shoe is still in good shape, you can often get a great deal.

Items to avoid buying used:

  • Water filters: Unless you can verify that the filter was properly cleaned and stored, it’s risky. A clogged or contaminated filter can make you sick. Buy these new.
  • Climbing ropes and harnesses: Never buy used safety-critical gear like ropes, harnesses, or carabiners. You don’t know their history—they might have been dropped, overloaded, or exposed to chemicals. Not worth the risk.
  • Helmets: Any helmet that has been impacted (even lightly) should be retired. Buy new.
  • Sleeping pads with leaks: Unless you’re prepared to patch them, avoid foam pads that are crushed or air pads with obvious holes. However, if the price is right and you’re handy with a patch kit, you can sometimes fix them.

Always ask for the age of the gear and how many nights it’s been used. A good rule of thumb: if the seller can’t answer basic questions about the item’s history, walk away.

The Art of Negotiation and Trade

Once you’ve found a piece of gear you like, don’t be afraid to negotiate. Most secondhand sellers expect a little haggling, especially at flea markets or gear swaps. Start with a respectful offer—usually 10–20% below the asking price—and be willing to walk away if you don’t reach a fair deal. If you’re buying online, ask if the seller can combine shipping or offer a discount for multiple items.

But negotiation isn’t just about money. You can also trade gear. At gear swaps, you can bring your old gear and swap it for something you need. On trail town forums, you might find someone willing to trade a tent for a stove. This is a great way to refresh your kit without spending a cent. I once traded a slightly-too-small backpack for a brand-new-looking pair of trekking poles plus a camp chair—both of which I used for the next 500 miles.

When trading, be honest about the condition of your gear. A reputation for fairness will make you welcome in the community, and you’ll be more likely to get good deals in the future.

Making Your Secondhand Gear Last

Once you’ve scored your pre-loved gear, the key to maximizing your investment is proper maintenance. Here are some tips to keep your secondhand gear in top shape:

  • Clean and reproof: Wash your tent with a specialized cleaner (like Nikwax Tent & Gear Cleaner) and reapply waterproofing if needed. For down sleeping bags, use a down-specific wash and dry on low with tennis balls to restore loft. For jackets, reapply DWR (durable water repellent) spray.
  • Repair small issues immediately: A tiny tear in your pack or tent can become a huge rip if ignored. Carry a small repair kit with Tenacious Tape, a needle and thread, and seam sealer. Fix zippers with a little zipper lubricant.
  • Store properly: Never store sleeping bags or jackets compressed. Hang them or store them loose in a cotton sack. Tents should be dry and loosely folded. This prevents permanent damage.
  • Learn basic gear repair: Knowing how to replace a broken zipper slider, patch a tent pole, or re-glue a sole can save you from having to replace the whole item. There are countless YouTube tutorials for common repairs.

By taking care of your secondhand gear, you’ll not only extend its life but also maintain its resale value. When you’re ready to upgrade, you can sell it and recoup some of your investment—keeping the cycle going.

Conclusion

The secondhand gear revolution is more than just a budget hack—it’s a mindset shift. It’s about recognizing that the best adventures don’t require the latest gear, only gear that works. By buying used, you save money, reduce waste, and become part of a community that values practicality over consumerism. So next time you’re tempted to drop $500 on a new sleeping bag, pause. Check the classifieds, visit a gear swap, or ask a fellow hiker. You might just find the perfect bag for $150—with a few trail stories already woven into its fabric. Happy trails, and happy hunting.
