The Lycian Way: Trekking Turkey's Turquoise Coast on a Shoestring

2026-06-10 · Treks · Tags: backpackerguide, treks

When most backpackers think of Turkey, they picture the bustling bazaars of Istanbul, the fairy chimneys of Cappadocia, or the white terraces of Pamukkale. But tucked along the southwestern coast lies a trekking gem that remains blissfully under the radar: the Lycian Way. Stretching over 500 kilometers from Ölüdeniz to Antalya, this ancient footpath weaves through pine forests, past crumbling Roman ruins, and along cliffs that drop into the turquoise Mediterranean. Best of all, it's a trail that can be tackled on a budget that would make even the most frugal traveler smile. I've walked it twice, and I'm here to show you how to experience the Lycian Way without breaking the bank—or your back.

Why the Lycian Way?

The Lycian Way isn't just a hike; it's a journey through history. The Lycians were an ancient civilization that left behind rock-cut tombs, amphitheaters, and fortified cities, many of which you'll pass directly on the trail. Unlike the crowded Inca Trail or the commercialized Annapurna Circuit, the Lycian Way offers solitude, dramatic coastal views, and a genuine sense of discovery. You'll walk through remote villages where shepherds still tend their flocks, and you'll swim in coves that are only accessible on foot. The trail is well-marked with red and white stripes, but it's not a manicured path—expect rocky sections, steep climbs, and occasional overgrowth. That's part of its charm.

For budget travelers, the Lycian Way is a dream. Turkey's lira has fluctuated, but even in peak season, you can get by on $30–40 per day if you're smart. That includes food, accommodation, and transport. Compare that to the $100+ per day you'd spend on the Alps or even the Dolomites, and you'll see why this trail is a steal.

Planning Your Route and Budget

The full Lycian Way takes about 25–30 days to walk, but you don't have to do it all. Many backpackers choose a section—the most popular being the stretch from Ölüdeniz to Kas, which takes 4–6 days and offers the best coastal scenery. Others start in Fethiye and head inland to the mountains around the Yedigöller (Seven Lakes) before dropping back to the sea. My advice: pick a section that matches your time and fitness level, and leave room for rest days in the beach towns.

Budget-wise, here's a rough breakdown:

  • Accommodation: Pensions and guesthouses in villages cost $10–15 per night for a private room, or $5–8 for a dorm. Wild camping is free, but you need to be self-sufficient and respectful of private land.
  • Food: Village markets sell fresh bread, cheese, olives, and tomatoes for a few dollars. A simple meal at a local restaurant (lokanta) runs $5–8. Cooking your own food can cut costs to $3–4 per day.
  • Transport: Buses and dolmuş (shared minibuses) are cheap—a ride between towns costs $2–5. You'll need to use them to get to trailheads and to skip boring road sections.
  • Gear: You don't need fancy equipment. A good pair of trail runners or lightweight boots, a 40–50L backpack, and a basic tent if you plan to camp. I'll detail gear below.

To keep costs low, plan to resupply in larger towns like Fethiye, Kas, and Demre. Stock up on non-perishables like nuts, dried fruit, and instant noodles. Water is available at springs and fountains in most villages, but always carry a purification method—I use a cheap Steripen or iodine tablets.

Getting There and Getting Around

Your starting point will likely be either Antalya or Dalaman, both with international airports. From there, take a bus to Fethiye or Ölüdeniz to begin the trail. If you're flying into Antalya, you can also start the trail from the eastern end, but most walkers go west to east to have the sun at their back and the sea views ahead.

Once on the trail, you'll rely on a mix of walking and public transport. The Lycian Way often passes through or near villages with dolmuş stops, so you can skip sections if you're tired or short on time. For example, the stretch from Alınca to Belceğiz is a long, waterless climb—many hikers take a dolmuş from the village of Çıralı to avoid it. Don't feel guilty; the trail is yours to customize.

Navigation is straightforward with the official Lycian Way map (available as a GPS app for a few dollars) or the paper map from the Arkeos Press. I recommend downloading the offline map on your phone—it's a lifesaver when the trail markers disappear after a wildfire or a new road cuts through. Also, pick up a copy of Kate Clow's guidebook if you can find it secondhand; it's the definitive resource.

Accommodation and Food on the Trail

One of the best things about the Lycian Way is the network of pensions and guesthouses that cater to hikers. These are often family-run, offering a warm welcome, a hearty meal, and a place to wash your clothes. In villages like Kabak, Faralya, and Alınca, you'll find basic rooms with shared bathrooms for around $10–15. Some places include breakfast—usually bread, jam, eggs, and tea—for an extra $3–5. It's worth it, as you'll need the calories.

If you're camping, you'll find plenty of flat spots, but be aware that much of the land is private or protected. Ask permission at a farmhouse or stick to designated campsites, which often charge a small fee ($3–5) and provide showers and toilets. Wild camping is possible in the pine forests, but always leave no trace and avoid lighting fires in summer.

Food on the trail is simple but delicious. In villages, you can buy fresh produce from local markets or roadside stands. Look for pomegranates, oranges, and figs in season. For a treat, try the local goat cheese and honey. When you're in a larger town, splurge on a proper Turkish breakfast—menemen (scrambled eggs with tomatoes and peppers), simit (sesame bread), and endless çay (tea). It's a cultural experience as much as a meal.

Essential Gear and Packing Tips

You don't need to invest in expensive ultralight gear for the Lycian Way. The trail is not technical, but it is rugged. Here's what I recommend:

  • Footwear: Trail runners are ideal—they're lighter, dry faster, and provide enough grip for the rocky paths. If you prefer ankle support, go with lightweight boots. Break them in before you go.
  • Backpack: A 40–50L pack is plenty. You'll be carrying water (up to 3L in dry sections), food, and a tent if you camp. Keep your base weight under 10kg.
  • Clothing: The coast is hot and humid in summer, but the mountains can be cool at night. Pack light, quick-dry layers, a fleece or down jacket for evenings, and a waterproof shell. A sun hat and sunglasses are essential.
  • Sleeping: If you're camping, a 3-season tent and a sleeping bag rated to 10°C (50°F) will suffice. Many pensions offer cheap dorm beds, so you might not need a tent at all.
  • Other: A headlamp, a power bank, a water filter or purification tablets, a small first-aid kit, and a whistle. Don't forget sunscreen and insect repellent—the mosquitoes can be fierce near water.

One tip: pack light on clothes. You'll be washing them by hand in village sinks or paying a few dollars for laundry service. A couple of shirts, one pair of hiking pants, and a pair of shorts are enough. The locals are used to scruffy hikers.

Trail Highlights and Challenges

The Lycian Way is not a walk in the park. Expect steep ascents and descents, loose scree, and sections that require scrambling. The heat can be brutal in July and August, so I recommend trekking in spring (April–May) or autumn (September–October) when temperatures are milder and wildflowers are in bloom. Winter brings snow to the higher passes, but the coastal sections are still walkable.

Some of my favorite moments:

  • Butterfly Valley: A stunning canyon that you can look down into from the trail above Faralya. It's a popular spot for cliff jumping and swimming, but the descent is steep and not for the faint-hearted.
  • Patara Beach: One of the longest beaches in Turkey, with ancient ruins at one end. You can camp nearby and watch the sunset over the Mediterranean.
  • Xanthos and Letoon: UNESCO World Heritage sites with impressive ruins. The trail passes right by them, so you can explore for free (or a small entrance fee).
  • Kas and Kalkan: Charming towns with a laid-back vibe. Take a rest day here to snorkel, kayak, or just eat your weight in meze.

The challenges are real, though. Water sources can be scarce in the summer—always check the map and carry extra. Trail markers sometimes disappear after forest fires, so have a GPS backup. And be prepared for the occasional aggressive dog; carry a stick or use a trekking pole to fend them off. But these obstacles are minor compared to the rewards.

Conclusion

The Lycian Way is a treasure trove for budget backpackers who crave adventure without the crowds. It offers a perfect blend of natural beauty, historical intrigue, and cultural immersion—all for a fraction of the cost of other European treks. Whether you walk a week or a month, you'll leave with memories of turquoise coves, ancient stones, and the warm hospitality of the Turkish people. So pack your bags, lace up your shoes, and hit the trail. The Lycian Way is waiting, and it won't break the bank.
