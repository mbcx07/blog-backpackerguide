The GR20 on a Budget: Corsica's Toughest Trek Without the Luxury Price

2026-06-24 · Treks · Tags: backpackerguide, The

The GR20 is often called Europe's toughest long-distance trek, a brutal 180-kilometer knife-edge across the granite spine of Corsica. It's a trail of sheer drops, scrambling, and breathtaking views that draws hikers from around the world. But for many, the dream of tackling this legendary route is quickly dashed by the perceived cost: flights to Corsica, pricey mountain refuges, and the need for specialized gear. The truth, however, is that the GR20 can be done on a shoestring budget without sacrificing the experience. With careful planning, a willingness to camp wild, and a bit of local savvy, you can conquer this alpine masterpiece for a fraction of what most tour operators charge. This guide will show you exactly how to hike the GR20 on a budget, from getting there to resupplying, so you can focus on the adventure, not the expense.

Why the GR20 is Worth the Effort

The GR20 traverses the island from north to south, starting in Calenzana and ending in Conca (or vice versa). It's not just a walk; it's a physical and mental challenge that rewards you with some of the most dramatic scenery in the Mediterranean. You'll cross high passes, traverse exposed ridges, and scramble over boulders, all while surrounded by jagged peaks and deep valleys. The trail is divided into 15 stages, but many hikers combine or split them to suit their pace. The sense of accomplishment at the end is immense, and the memories of sunsets over the sea from a mountain bivouac are priceless. But the real beauty is that you don't need to spend a fortune to experience this. The GR20 is a public trail, and the only mandatory costs are your transport to the trailheads and your food. Everything else is negotiable.

Getting to Corsica and the Trailheads on the Cheap

The biggest hurdle for many is the flight to Corsica. The island has two main airports: Ajaccio and Bastia, but you can also arrive by ferry from mainland France or Italy. To save money, consider flying into a nearby city like Nice or Marseille and taking a ferry. The ferry is often cheaper than a direct flight, especially if you book in advance. For example, a ferry from Nice to Calvi (near the northern trailhead) can cost as little as €30 if you book early and travel as a foot passenger. From Calvi, you can take a local bus to Calenzana, the traditional start. Alternatively, if you fly into Bastia, you can take a train to Calvi and then a bus. The key is to be flexible with your dates and use budget airlines like Ryanair or EasyJet that fly into Bastia or Ajaccio from various European cities. Once on the island, public transport is limited but workable. Buses run between major towns, and you can often hitchhike to trailheads—a common practice among budget trekkers. Just be prepared to walk a few extra kilometers if the bus doesn't go all the way.

Accommodation: Refuges vs. Wild Camping

The GR20 is dotted with mountain refuges, which offer dormitory beds, meals, and sometimes showers. These are convenient but pricey: a bed costs around €15-20 per night, and a full meal (dinner and breakfast) can set you back €25-30. If you're on a strict budget, you'll want to avoid these as much as possible. The good news is that wild camping is allowed along the GR20, with a few restrictions. You can pitch your tent almost anywhere, as long as you're not in a protected area or too close to a refuge. Many hikers choose to camp for free and only use the refuges for water and maybe a hot meal every few days. This can cut your accommodation costs to zero. However, you need to be self-sufficient: carry a lightweight tent, a sleeping bag, and a stove. The refuges also have water sources, but you'll need to filter or treat it. If you do want to splurge occasionally, pick a refuge with a good reputation for food—like the one at Manganu, which serves hearty Corsican dishes. But for the most part, camping is the way to go for budget trekkers.

Food and Resupply: Eating Well Without Breaking the Bank

Food is another major expense on the GR20 if you rely on refuge meals. Instead, plan to carry your own food and resupply at the few villages that the trail passes near. The GR20 crosses or comes close to several towns: Calenzana, Asco, Castel di Verghio, Vizzavona, and Conca. These are your resupply points. You can buy pasta, rice, instant oats, dried fruits, nuts, and other lightweight staples at local supermarkets. The prices are reasonable, especially if you buy in bulk. For example, a 1kg bag of pasta costs about €1.50, and a jar of pesto is €2. You can also find fresh bread and cheese in some villages, which make for a luxurious trail lunch. To save even more, consider dehydrating your own meals before you go, but that's not necessary if you're willing to eat simple. A typical day's food might cost €5-7 if you buy from supermarkets. Compare that to €30 for refuge meals, and you'll see the savings. Just remember to carry enough for the stretches between resupply points, which can be 3-4 days. Also, always carry a water filter or purification tablets, as you'll be drinking from streams and springs.

Gear and Packing: Lighten Your Load and Your Wallet

You don't need to buy expensive ultralight gear to hike the GR20 on a budget. The key is to pack only what you need and to make smart choices. A good pair of hiking boots with ankle support is essential, but you don't need the latest Gore-Tex model. Look for sales or buy used gear from online marketplaces. A lightweight tent can be rented or borrowed, or you can use a tarp and bivy sack if you're experienced. Your sleeping bag should be rated for around 0°C (32°F) because nights can be cold at altitude. A simple stove, like a pocket rocket, and a small pot are enough for cooking. You'll also need a headlamp, a map, and a compass (or a GPS app on your phone, but keep it offline). The most important thing is to keep your pack weight under 15kg (33 lbs) to make the climbs manageable. Every extra kilo costs you energy and potentially money if you need to buy gear on the trail. So, before you go, lay out all your gear and ask yourself if you really need it. A good rule is to bring only what you use every day. For example, leave the camp chair at home—you can sit on a rock. And instead of buying expensive energy bars, make your own trail mix with peanuts, raisins, and chocolate chips.

Budget Breakdown and Money-Saving Tips

So, how much does a budget GR20 trek actually cost? Let's break it down. Assuming you're coming from mainland Europe, your biggest costs are transport and food. A round-trip ferry from Nice to Calvi can cost around €60-80 if booked in advance. If you fly, a budget airline ticket might be €50-100 each way, but you'll need to add airport transfers. On the island, buses and trains are cheap—maybe €20 total for all your transfers. For food, if you buy from supermarkets and cook your own meals, you can budget €10-12 per day. That's about €150-180 for a 15-day trek. If you stay in refuges for a few nights, add €20-30 per night. But if you camp every night, your accommodation cost is zero. So, a realistic budget for a 15-day trek is around €300-400, excluding your travel to the island. That's incredibly affordable for one of Europe's most iconic treks. To save even more, consider going in the shoulder season (June or September) when prices are lower and the trail is less crowded. Also, team up with other hikers to share food and stove fuel. And don't forget to check if your travel insurance covers trekking—it's a must, but you can find affordable policies online.

Conclusion

The GR20 is a bucket-list trek for many, but it doesn't have to be a financial burden. By camping wild, cooking your own meals, and using public transport, you can experience the raw beauty of Corsica's mountains for a fraction of the cost of a guided tour. The trail itself is free, and the memories you'll make are priceless. So, pack your tent, grab your map, and set off on an adventure that proves you don't need deep pockets to conquer Europe's toughest trail. The mountains are waiting, and they're more accessible than you think.
