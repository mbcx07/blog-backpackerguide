The Annapurna Circuit on a Shoestring: Nepal's Iconic Trek Without the Tourist Tax

2026-05-12 · Destinations · Tags: The, destinations, backpackerguide

The Annapurna Circuit has a reputation as one of the great treks of the world—and an equally strong reputation as one of the more expensive ones. Between permits, guides, teahouse stays, and the inevitable gear upgrades, it's easy to see why many budget travelers write it off as a once-in-a-lifetime splurge. But here's the truth: the Annapurna Circuit can be done for a fraction of what most people spend, and you don't have to sacrifice comfort or safety to do it. With a little planning and a willingness to walk your own path, this Himalayan classic becomes one of the most affordable adventures on the planet.

Why the Annapurna Circuit Still Matters

The Annapurna Circuit is not just a trek; it's a journey through some of the most diverse landscapes on Earth. In two to three weeks, you'll pass through subtropical forests, terraced rice paddies, alpine meadows, and the arid Tibetan plateau. You'll cross Thorong La Pass at 5,416 meters, one of the highest trekking passes in the world, and you'll do it all while staying in family-run teahouses that have been welcoming trekkers for decades.

The circuit was once the most popular trek in Nepal, and while the road has changed the experience, it hasn't diminished the magic. The trail still offers incredible views of Annapurna I, Dhaulagiri, Machapuchare, and a dozen other peaks over 7,000 meters. And because the road now reaches further into the mountains, you have more flexibility than ever to customize your trek to your budget and timeline. Whether you have two weeks or a full month, the circuit can be shaped to fit your pace and your wallet.

Getting There: Budget Transport to the Trailhead

Most trekkers start the Annapurna Circuit in Besisahar, a small town about a six-hour bus ride from Kathmandu. The local bus from Kathmandu's Gongabu Bus Station to Besisahar costs around 500-700 NPR (roughly $4-6 USD) and departs several times daily. It's not luxurious—expect cramped seats, winding roads, and the occasional goat on the roof—but it's an authentic Nepali experience and a fraction of the cost of a private jeep.

From Besisahar, you can either start walking immediately or take a local jeep further up the valley to save time. The road now extends all the way to Manang, and you can negotiate a shared jeep ride for around 1,500-2,000 NPR per person. If you're on a tight budget, walking from Besisahar is the way to go—it adds three to four days to your trek but saves you money and lets you experience the lower villages that many trekkers skip. These lower sections are where you'll find the most authentic teahouse experiences, with families who have been hosting trekkers for generations and still charge prices that feel like a gift.

Daily Costs: What to Expect on the Trail

Here's the good news: the Annapurna Circuit is one of the most affordable treks in the world if you know how to play the game. Teahouse accommodation is remarkably cheap—typically 200-500 NPR ($2-4 USD) per night for a basic room with a bed and blankets. In many teahouses, the room is free or nearly free if you eat your meals there, which is the standard arrangement. Meals are also affordable:

  • Dal bhat (the traditional Nepali lentil and rice dish): 300-500 NPR ($2.50-4 USD) with unlimited refills in most places
  • Momos, thukpa, and fried rice: 200-400 NPR ($1.50-3.50 USD)
  • Hot drinks and tea: 100-200 NPR, though prices climb with altitude

The biggest expense on the trail is drinks—hot water, tea, and bottled drinks get progressively more expensive as you gain altitude. A liter of hot water can cost 200-300 NPR at higher elevations, and a can of Coke might set you back 400 NPR. Budget for 1,500-2,500 NPR ($12-20 USD) per day for food and lodging, and you'll eat well and sleep comfortably. That's less than the cost of a single restaurant meal in most Western cities.

Teahouse Strategies: Eating and Sleeping for Less

The teahouse system is the backbone of the Annapurna Circuit, and understanding how it works is key to keeping your costs down. The unwritten rule is that you stay where you eat—teahouses make their money on meals, not rooms, so they expect you to have dinner and breakfast at their establishment. This is a fair trade: you get a cheap room, and they get a steady stream of customers.

To make the most of this system, plan your day so you arrive at your teahouse by mid-afternoon. This gives you time to settle in, order a hot drink, and enjoy the afternoon light before dinner. It also means you'll have your pick of rooms, which matters at higher elevations where space is limited. Another tip: order dal bhat for dinner. It's the cheapest meal on the menu, it's filling, and the refill policy means you'll never go hungry. Many trekkers also carry a small supply of snacks—nuts, chocolate, energy bars—to supplement their meals and avoid paying teahouse prices for treats. A reusable water bottle with a purification filter is another smart investment; buying bottled water every day adds up fast, and the streams along the circuit are generally safe to filter.

Thorong La Pass: The High Point Without the High Price

Crossing Thorong La Pass is the highlight of the Annapurna Circuit, and it's also where many trekkers overspend. The standard approach is to stay in Manang (3,519m) for an acclimatization day, then hike to Thorong Phedi (4,540m) or High Camp (4,850m) the day before the crossing. The teahouses at Thorong Phedi and High Camp are more expensive than those lower down—rooms cost 500-800 NPR and meals are pricier—but they're still affordable if you plan ahead.

The key is to carry enough water and snacks to avoid buying overpriced drinks at the pass itself. The crossing itself is a long, demanding day—you'll start before dawn and spend 7-9 hours above 4,500 meters—but the sense of achievement when you reach the prayer flags at the top is worth every step. After the pass, you descend to Muktinath, a sacred pilgrimage site, and then continue to Jomsom, where you can catch a bus or flight back to Pokhara. If you're feeling strong, consider walking the final stretch to Jomsom rather than taking a jeep—the windswept Kali Gandaki Valley has a stark beauty that's easy to miss from a vehicle.

Side Treks and Extensions That Won't Break the Bank

One of the great things about the Annapurna Circuit is the number of side treks and extensions you can add without blowing your budget. The most popular is the trek to Tilicho Lake (4,919m), the highest lake in the world, which adds three to four days to your itinerary. The trail to Tilicho is challenging—there's a section with a steep cliffside path that requires careful footing—but the reward is a stunning turquoise lake surrounded by snow-capped peaks.

Another option is the Poon Hill extension, which you can add at the end of the circuit if you're heading toward Pokhara. Poon Hill is famous for its sunrise views over the Annapurna and Dhaulagiri ranges, and the trek to the viewpoint is a relatively easy two-day add-on. Both side treks use the same teahouse system, so your daily costs remain the same. Just be sure to factor in the extra days when planning your budget and your visa. And if you're traveling in the shoulder seasons—March to April or October to November—you'll find teahouses even more willing to negotiate on room rates, as they're eager to fill beds during the quieter weeks.

Conclusion

The Annapurna Circuit is proof that the world's greatest adventures don't have to come with a hefty price tag. With careful planning, a flexible attitude, and a willingness to embrace the teahouse way of life, you can trek one of the most iconic routes on Earth for less than you'd spend on a weekend in a major city. The mountains don't care how much you paid to see them—they'll reward you with the same breathtaking views, the same warm hospitality, and the same sense of accomplishment whether you're sleeping in a budget teahouse or a luxury lodge. So pack your bags, lace up your boots, and get ready for the trek of a lifetime. The Annapurna Circuit is waiting, and it's more affordable than you think.
