The West Highland Way: Scotland's Highland Adventure Without the High Costs

2026-05-26 · Destinations · Tags: The, backpackerguide, destinations

Scotland's West Highland Way is a 96-mile (154 km) trail that winds from the outskirts of Glasgow to the foot of Ben Nevis in Fort William. It's a rite of passage for many hikers, offering a dramatic shift from lowland lochs and rolling farmland to the rugged, moody Highlands. But for budget-conscious backpackers, the fear of Scottish prices—think £15 pints and £100 B&Bs—can be daunting. The good news? With a bit of planning, the West Highland Way is not only doable on a shoestring, but it can also be one of the most rewarding, cost-effective treks in Europe. This guide breaks down exactly how to hike the Way without blowing your travel fund, from free camping spots to supermarket resupply strategies.

Why the West Highland Way Belongs on Your Budget Trek List

The West Highland Way is often overshadowed by more famous long-distance trails like the Camino de Santiago or the Tour du Mont Blanc. But for backpackers, it's a hidden gem. The trail is well-marked, relatively easy to navigate, and offers a mix of terrains that keeps every day interesting. You'll walk along the bonnie banks of Loch Lomond, cross the desolate Rannoch Moor, and climb the Devil's Staircase before descending into the outdoor capital of the UK. Best of all, the trail is completely free to walk—no permits, no fees, no reservations required. You can start any day, any time, and go at your own pace. That's a huge win for budget travelers who want flexibility without the administrative hassle.

What's more, the trail passes through several towns and villages where you can resupply cheaply. Unlike remote treks in Scandinavia or the Alps, you're never more than a day's walk from a supermarket or a pub. This means you can carry less food and water, saving weight and money. And with a bit of creativity, you can keep your daily costs down to £20–£30, including food, accommodation (if you're not wild camping), and the occasional pint.

Getting There and Getting Around: Cheap Transport Hacks

Your journey starts in Milngavie, a suburb of Glasgow. The easiest and cheapest way to get there is by train from Glasgow Queen Street station to Milngavie—a 20-minute ride that costs around £4 if you book in advance or use a railcard. If you're flying into Glasgow or Edinburgh, take the airport bus into the city center (around £8) and then hop on the train. For the return trip, Fort William is well-connected by bus and train. The ScotRail train from Fort William to Glasgow takes about 3.5 hours and can be as cheap as £15 if you book a few days ahead on the Advance fare. Alternatively, the Citylink bus runs daily and is often cheaper, especially if you book online. To save even more, consider hiking the trail in the shoulder season (April–May or September–October) when transport and accommodation prices drop, and the midges are less ferocious.

If you're coming from outside the UK, look into a BritRail pass or a Youth Hostel Association (YHA) membership, which can give you discounts on transport and hostels. Also, check for group deals if you're traveling with friends—some bus companies offer 2-for-1 or group discounts. And don't forget to use apps like Trainline or Busbud to compare prices and snag last-minute deals.

Sleeping for Less: Hostels, Bothies, and Wild Camping

Accommodation is where you can save the most on the West Highland Way. The trail is dotted with hostels, bunkhouses, and campsites, but prices can vary wildly. A bed in a hostel dorm typically costs £20–£30 per night, which is reasonable, but you can cut that down to zero by wild camping. Scotland has a unique right-to-roam law that allows you to camp on most unenclosed land, as long as you follow the Scottish Outdoor Access Code. This means you can pitch your tent almost anywhere along the trail, provided you're not in a field with crops or too close to a house. The most popular wild camping spots are along Loch Lomond, but be aware that there are restrictions in some areas—look for signs or check the Loch Lomond & The Trossachs National Park website for camping management zones. In these zones, you may need a permit (which is free) or you must use designated campsites. Still, it's a fantastic way to save money and wake up to stunning views.

If you prefer a roof over your head, consider staying in bothies—simple, unmanned shelters maintained by the Mountain Bothies Association. There are a few along the route, like the one at Doune Byre or the one near Kingshouse, but they're basic (no toilets, no water) and first-come, first-served. For a more comfortable night, look for YHAs (Youth Hostels) in places like Rowardennan, Crianlarich, and Glen Nevis. They offer dorm beds, self-catering kitchens, and often have drying rooms—essential for the inevitable Scottish rain. To save money, book in advance and consider a YHA membership (around £15 per year) which gives you 10% off. Also, many hostels offer a free night after a certain number of stays, so if you're planning a longer trip, it's worth joining.

Fueling the Hike: Food and Resupply on a Budget

Food is another area where you can keep costs low. The trail passes through several towns with supermarkets: Milngavie (start), Drymen, Balmaha, Rowardennan (small shop), Inversnaid (hotel shop), Crianlarich, Tyndrum, Bridge of Orchy, Kingshouse (hotel), Kinlochleven, and Fort William. The best strategy is to buy bulk staples—pasta, rice, oats, dried fruit, nuts, and instant meals—at the big supermarkets in Milngavie or Fort William before you start. Then, only buy fresh items like bread, cheese, and fruit as you pass through towns. This way, you avoid the inflated prices at tourist shops and hotels. For example, a loaf of bread at a hotel shop might cost £3, but at a Co-op in Tyndrum, it's £1.20.

For hot meals, carry a lightweight stove and cook your own dinner. Many hostels and campsites have cooking facilities, but if you're wild camping, you'll need your own. A simple canister stove and a pot are enough. For lunch, consider making your own trail mix and peanut butter sandwiches. And don't forget to treat yourself to a pub meal at least once—it's part of the experience. Look for 'two-for-one' deals or early bird specials, which are common in rural pubs. A hearty pub lunch can cost £8–£12, but it's worth it for the atmosphere and a break from instant noodles.

Gear and Packing: Lighten Your Load and Your Wallet

You don't need expensive, high-end gear to hike the West Highland Way. In fact, the trail is forgiving enough that you can get away with budget equipment, as long as you're prepared for rain. The key is to pack light and layer up. A good waterproof jacket and trousers are non-negotiable—you will get wet. But you don't need a £300 Gore-Tex shell; a decent £50 rain jacket from a budget brand like Decathlon will do the job. Similarly, a pair of sturdy walking shoes or trail runners are fine; you don't need heavy boots. The trail is well-maintained, and the biggest challenge is the mud and rocky sections, so good grip is more important than ankle support.

For your pack, aim for under 10 kg (22 lbs) including food and water. This will make the hiking easier and reduce fatigue. Invest in a good backpack that fits well—you can find used ones online or at gear swaps. For sleeping, a lightweight tent (under 2 kg) and a sleeping bag rated for 0°C (32°F) are sufficient. If you're planning to use hostels, you can skip the tent and just bring a sleeping bag liner. Remember, you can always buy gear in Glasgow at outdoor shops like Tiso or Cotswold Outdoor, but you'll pay retail prices. Instead, check out charity shops (thrift stores) in Glasgow for fleeces and waterproofs—you might find a bargain.

Trail Highlights and Side Trips: Making the Most of Your Time

The West Highland Way is not just about the walking; it's about the experiences. Here are some must-see spots that won't cost you a penny (or very little):

  • Conic Hill (near Balmaha): A short but steep climb that offers panoramic views over Loch Lomond and the islands. It's a great spot for a picnic.
  • Rannoch Moor: This vast, empty expanse feels like another planet. It's a highlight for many hikers, especially at sunrise or sunset. Just be prepared for boggy sections.
  • The Devil's Staircase: A steep ascent that rewards you with views of Glencoe and the Black Mount. It's tough but worth it.
  • Kinlochleven: A former aluminium smelting village with a great ice factor (climbing wall) and a pub that serves excellent fish and chips. You can also visit the Grey Mare's Tail waterfall for free.
  • Ben Nevis: If you have an extra day, you can summit the UK's highest mountain (1,345 m) from Fort William. It's a challenging but free hike, and the views are epic. Just check the weather and be prepared for a long day.

To save money on side trips, skip the tourist buses and walk. The trail itself is the main attraction, and you'll find that the best moments are often the unplanned ones—a chat with a fellow hiker, a swim in a loch, or a quiet moment watching the deer.

Conclusion

The West Highland Way is proof that you don't need a fat wallet to experience one of the world's most beautiful landscapes. With free camping, budget-friendly hostels, and smart food planning, you can hike this iconic trail for under £30 a day. The real cost is in the memories—the misty mornings on Rannoch Moor, the laughter in a bothy, the sense of achievement as you reach Fort William. So pack your rain gear, grab your map, and hit the trail. Scotland's Highlands are waiting, and they're more affordable than you think.
