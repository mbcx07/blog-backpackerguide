The Kungsleden on a Shoestring: Trekking Sweden's Arctic Trail Without Breaking the Bank

2026-07-11 · Destinations · Tags: destinations, The

There’s a certain magic to the Arctic Circle—a raw, silent vastness that makes you feel both infinitesimal and utterly alive. The Kungsleden, or the King’s Trail, stretches 440 kilometers through Swedish Lapland, from Abisko in the north to Hemavan in the south. It’s a trek that promises midnight sun, birch forests, and glacier-carved valleys, but many assume it’s a pricey expedition reserved for the well-heeled. The truth? With a bit of savvy, you can hike this iconic trail on a budget that would make a hostel dorm look luxurious. I’ve walked it twice—once on a shoestring, once with a bit more comfort—and I’m here to tell you that the Arctic doesn’t have to cost a fortune. Here’s how to conquer the Kungsleden without emptying your savings.

Why the Kungsleden Belongs on Your Bucket List

The Kungsleden isn’t just a trail; it’s a rite of passage for backpackers who crave solitude and raw nature. Unlike the crowded peaks of the Alps or the Inca Trail, this route offers a sense of remoteness that’s increasingly rare. You’ll traverse birch forests that turn golden in autumn, cross rushing rivers on footbridges, and climb barren tundra where reindeer roam. The trail is well-marked and maintained, with mountain huts spaced roughly a day’s walk apart—a boon for budget travelers who can skip expensive guided tours and go solo.

What makes it even more appealing is its accessibility. The northern terminus, Abisko, is reachable by train from Stockholm, and the trail itself is free to hike. The only costs are transport, food, and accommodation—and all three can be minimized with a little planning. Whether you’re a seasoned trekker or a first-timer, the Kungsleden offers a perfect balance of challenge and reward, all without the price tag of a guided expedition.

Planning Your Budget: The Real Costs

Let’s break down the numbers. A typical 7-10 day section hike from Abisko to Nikkaluokta (the most popular stretch) can be done for under $300 if you’re frugal. Here’s where your money goes:

  • Transport: A round-trip train from Stockholm to Abisko costs around $100 if you book in advance (SJ’s discount tickets). From Nikkaluokta, a bus back to Kiruna adds $20.
  • Accommodation: Mountain huts run about $30 per night for STF members (non-members pay $40). Camping is free, but you’ll need a tent and a stove.
  • Food: Plan on $10-15 per day for dehydrated meals, oats, and trail mix. You can resupply at Abisko, Kebnekaise, and a few other points.
  • Gear: If you already have a tent and sleeping bag, you’re set. If not, rent in Abisko for about $50 for the week.

The biggest budget killer is impulse spending at the mountain huts—they sell snacks and drinks at premium prices. Avoid that by packing your own. Also, consider hiking in June or September, when hut prices drop slightly and crowds thin out.

Getting There and Resupplying Like a Pro

Your journey begins in Abisko, a tiny village that’s surprisingly well-connected. The overnight train from Stockholm is a budget traveler’s dream—you save a night’s accommodation and wake up in the Arctic. Book your ticket at least a month in advance to snag the cheapest seat (around $50 if you’re flexible). Once in Abisko, stock up at the STF station’s small shop, but beware: prices are high. Instead, bring your own dehydrated meals from home or buy them in Stockholm’s budget supermarkets like Lidl or Willys.

Resupply points are sparse but strategic. The most common resupply is at Kebnekaise Mountain Station, about halfway through the Abisko-Nikkaluokta section. They have a small store with basic supplies, but again, it’s pricey. A better trick is to mail a food drop to yourself at the STF hut in Sälka or Singi—some huts accept packages for a small fee. Or, simply plan your food to last the entire stretch; with a bit of creativity, you can carry 7 days of food without breaking your back. Dehydrated meals are lightweight, and you can supplement with foraged berries (lingonberries and cloudberries are abundant in late summer).

Sleeping on the Cheap: Huts vs. Camping

The Kungsleden offers two main accommodation options: the iconic red STF huts and wild camping. Huts are cozy, with bunk beds, a stove, and sometimes a sauna—but they cost money. Camping, on the other hand, is completely free, and Sweden’s Right of Public Access (allemansrätten) allows you to pitch your tent almost anywhere, as long as you’re 200 meters from a hut or cabin. This is your golden ticket to a budget trek.

If you’re a seasoned camper, skip the huts entirely. You’ll save $200 over a week, and you’ll have the freedom to stop wherever the view takes your breath away. The trail is dotted with designated camping spots, often near streams or lakes, with basic facilities like a fire pit and a toilet. Just remember to bring a reliable tent—the Arctic wind is no joke—and a stove for cooking. If you do want a hut experience, pick a few nights to splurge, especially if the weather turns. A night in a hut after a storm is worth every krona.

One insider tip: join the Swedish Tourist Association (STF) before you go. Membership costs about $50 and gives you discounts on hut stays, plus access to their network of cabins. If you plan to use huts for even three nights, the membership pays for itself.

Gear and Food: Lighten Your Load, Lighten Your Wallet

You don’t need fancy ultralight gear to hike the Kungsleden, but you do need to be smart. The trail is well-maintained, but the weather can change in minutes—sun to sleet to snow, even in July. Your gear list should prioritize warmth and waterproofing over weight. A good rain jacket, insulated layers, and sturdy boots are non-negotiable. But you can save money by renting gear in Abisko rather than buying it. The STF station rents tents, sleeping bags, and stoves at reasonable rates, and you can return them at the end of your hike.

Food is where you can really cut costs. Dehydrated meals from brands like Real Turmat are convenient but pricey (around $8 each). Instead, make your own: instant mashed potatoes, couscous, and ramen noodles are cheap and lightweight. Add dried vegetables, nuts, and chocolate for energy. For breakfast, oats with powdered milk and dried fruit are a classic. And don’t forget to pack a small bottle of oil—it’s calorie-dense and can transform a bland meal. Plan for about 2,500 calories per day, and you’ll stay fueled without overspending.

Another budget hack: bring a water filter. The streams and lakes along the trail are crystal clear, but you’ll want to filter to avoid giardia. A lightweight filter like the Sawyer Mini costs $30 and saves you from buying bottled water (which is heavy and expensive). You’ll also want to carry a small repair kit—duct tape, a needle, and a patch kit—to fix any gear mishaps on the trail.

Trail Highlights and Insider Tips

The Abisko to Nikkaluokta section is the crown jewel of the Kungsleden, and for good reason. You’ll pass through the stunning Lapporten valley, cross the Tjaktjajaure lake by boat (a free ferry included in your STF membership), and get a close-up view of Sweden’s highest peak, Kebnekaise. The trail is well-marked with red poles, and the terrain is mostly gentle—perfect for beginners. But don’t underestimate the river crossings; some are bridged, but others require careful wading. Check the water levels at the huts before you set out.

For a truly budget experience, consider hiking the southern section from Hemavan to Ammarnäs. It’s less crowded, and the huts are cheaper. You’ll also find more wild camping spots, and the landscape is equally dramatic—think rolling tundra and birch forests. If you’re short on time, the Abisko to Kebnekaise stretch (about 3 days) is a perfect weekend adventure.

One of my favorite memories was sitting by a glacial stream at midnight, the sun still high in the sky, watching a herd of reindeer cross the valley. That moment cost me nothing but a few hours of walking. The Kungsleden is a reminder that the best things in life—and in travel—are often free. So pack your tent, skip the guided tour, and let the Arctic work its magic on your soul—and your wallet.

Conclusion

The Kungsleden is proof that epic adventures don’t require a fat bank account. With careful planning, a willingness to camp, and a bit of DIY food prep, you can trek through one of Europe’s last great wildernesses for less than the cost of a weekend in a city. The trail offers solitude, stunning landscapes, and a sense of accomplishment that no luxury resort can match. So go ahead—book that train ticket, lace up your boots, and discover why the King’s Trail is a budget backpacker’s dream. The Arctic is waiting, and it’s more affordable than you think.
