The Carretera Austral: Chile's Wild Patagonian Highway on a Budget

2026-08-07 · Destinations · Tags: The, destinations

There are roads that take you from one place to another, and then there are roads that become the journey itself. The Carretera Austral is the latter. Stretching roughly 1,240 kilometers through the wild heart of Chilean Patagonia, this mostly unpaved highway cuts through fjords, temperate rainforests, turquoise rivers, and jagged granite peaks. It's a route that demands patience, flexibility, and a willingness to embrace the unexpected. And for backpackers willing to trade comfort for adventure, it's one of the most rewarding—and surprisingly affordable—destinations in South America.

Why the Carretera Austral Belongs on Your Bucket List

Unlike the well-trodden gringo trail of Peru and Bolivia, the Carretera Austral remains refreshingly off the radar. You won't find tour buses disgorging crowds at every viewpoint. Instead, you'll share the road with local farmers in pickup trucks, the occasional cyclist hauling months of gear, and the odd hitchhiking backpacker who's become your temporary travel companion. The region is vast, sparsely populated, and gloriously untamed. Towns are small, distances are long, and the landscape changes dramatically around every bend—from emerald-green valleys to glacial lakes the color of milky turquoise.

What makes it even more appealing is the cost. While Patagonia has a reputation for being expensive, the Carretera Austral is a different story. With a tent, a stove, and a bit of planning, you can comfortably travel here on $30 to $40 a day. That's a fraction of what you'd spend in Torres del Paine or El Chaltén, and you'll have far more of the wilderness to yourself.

Getting There and Getting Around

The Carretera Austral officially begins in Puerto Montt, a city in Chile's Lake District, and ends in the tiny frontier settlement of Villa O'Higgins, near the Argentine border. Most backpackers start in Puerto Montt and head south, which makes sense logistically—you can fly into Puerto Montt from Santiago, or cross over from Argentina via Bariloche.

Once you're on the road, your options are buses, hitchhiking, or renting a car. Buses run along the main route but are infrequent, especially in the southern stretches. The good news is that hitchhiking is not only common but practically the norm here. Locals are used to picking up travelers, and you'll often find yourself in the back of a pickup truck, wind in your hair, watching the landscape roll by. It's free, it's social, and it's part of the experience. That said, don't rely solely on your thumb—carry a tent and enough food for a few days, because you never know when the next ride will come.

If you have a bit more budget, renting a car with a few other travelers can be a great option. Splitting the cost of a small 4x4 among three or four people makes it surprisingly reasonable, and it gives you the freedom to stop wherever you want. Just be prepared for gravel roads, river crossings, and the occasional flat tire.

The Stops That Make the Journey Worth It

The Carretera Austral isn't about the destination—it's about the string of incredible places you'll pass along the way. Here are the ones you absolutely shouldn't skip.

Pumalín Park is the first major stop south of Puerto Montt. This private nature reserve, founded by the late conservationist Douglas Tompkins, protects a vast swath of temperate rainforest. The park's network of trails is well-maintained and free to enter, and the highlight is the short hike to the base of the Chaitén volcano, which erupted dramatically in 2008. The park's campgrounds are excellent and cheap, making it a perfect first night on the road.

Further south, Futaleufú is a small town that punches well above its weight. It's famous for the Río Futaleufú, one of the best whitewater rivers in the world. Even if you're not into rafting, the river's impossibly blue water and the surrounding mountains make it worth a stop. Rafting trips are pricey, but you can hike along the river and swim in the calmer sections for free.

Coyhaique is the largest town on the route and your best bet for resupplying. Stock up on food, fuel, and cash here—ATMs are scarce further south. It's not the prettiest town, but it's a practical hub, and the nearby Reserva Nacional Cerro Castillo is the real draw. The trek to the base of Cerro Castillo's jagged peaks is one of the best in Patagonia, and it's completely free to hike.

Just past Coyhaique, don't miss Puerto Río Tranquilo, where you can take a boat tour to the famous marble caves (Capillas de Mármol) on Lake General Carrera. The boat tours are relatively inexpensive, and the swirling blue and white marble formations are genuinely stunning. If you're on a tight budget, you can also kayak to the caves, which is even more memorable.

Finally, the road ends at Villa O'Higgins, a tiny settlement that feels like the edge of the world. From here, you can hike to the Argentine border and connect to El Chaltén, or simply soak in the silence and the staggering views of the Southern Patagonian Ice Field. It's a fitting end to an epic journey.

Trekking Highlights Along the Route

While the drive itself is spectacular, the Carretera Austral is also a gateway to some of the best trekking in South America. The Cerro Castillo Trek is the standout. This multi-day hike takes you through lenga forests, past glacial lakes, and up to a high pass with jaw-dropping views of the castle-like peak that gives the mountain its name. It's challenging but doable for most fit backpackers, and you can camp for free along the route.

Another highlight is Queulat National Park, home to the famous hanging glacier. The short hike to the viewpoint is steep but rewarding, and the sight of a glacier clinging to a sheer cliff face, with chunks of ice calving into the lake below, is unforgettable. The park also has several longer trails if you want to go deeper into the wilderness.

For something truly off the beaten path, consider the Exploradores Glacier near Puerto Río Tranquilo. You can hike to the glacier's edge without a guide, and the views of the ice field stretching to the horizon are awe-inspiring. If you have the budget, a guided ice hike is worth every penny, but even the free viewpoint is spectacular.

Budget Breakdown: How to Make It Work

Here's the thing about the Carretera Austral: it's as expensive or as cheap as you make it. The biggest costs are transport and accommodation, but both can be minimized with a little planning.

  • Accommodation: Camping is your best friend. Most towns have cheap or free campgrounds, and wild camping is generally tolerated in remote areas. Hostels and hospedajes (family-run guesthouses) cost around $10–15 per night if you want a bed and a hot shower.
  • Food: Grocery stores are limited in small towns, so stock up in Coyhaique. Pasta, rice, lentils, canned goods, and bread are your staples. Cooking your own meals will keep your daily food costs under $10. Eating out is possible but expensive, so save it for a treat.
  • Transport: Buses are the most reliable option, but they add up. Hitchhiking is free and widely accepted. If you're splitting a rental car, budget around $20–30 per person per day, including fuel.
  • Activities: The marble caves boat tour is around $15–20. Rafting in Futaleufú is $50–80. Most treks are free. Pick and choose your splurges wisely.

All told, a realistic daily budget is $30–40 if you're camping and cooking, or $50–60 if you're mixing in hostels and the occasional tour. That's remarkably good value for one of the most spectacular regions on Earth.

Practical Tips for a Smooth Journey

The Carretera Austral is not a place to wing it entirely. A little preparation goes a long way. First, time your visit. The best months are November through March, when the weather is warmest and the days are longest. Even then, expect rain and wind—this is Patagonia, after all. Pack a quality rain jacket, waterproof boots, and layers.

Second, carry cash. ATMs are few and far between, and many small towns don't have one at all. Withdraw a healthy amount in Coyhaique or Puerto Montt before heading south. Credit cards are rarely accepted outside of larger towns.

Third, learn some Spanish. English is not widely spoken in this part of Chile, and a few basic phrases will go a long way. Locals are friendly and helpful, but they'll appreciate the effort.

Finally, be flexible. Buses get delayed, weather changes plans, and the road sometimes closes due to landslides. Embrace the unpredictability. Some of the best moments on the Carretera Austral are the ones you never planned for—a spontaneous invitation to a family barbecue, a hidden waterfall you stumbled upon, a sunset that turned the mountains pink.

Conclusion

The Carretera Austral is more than a road trip—it's a rite of passage for the adventurous backpacker. It's a place where the journey truly is the destination, where every kilometer brings a new landscape, and where the simplicity of life on the road strips away the noise of the modern world. It's not the easiest trip you'll ever take, but it's almost certainly one of the most rewarding. So pack your tent, hitch a ride, and let the wild road take you where it will. You won't regret it.
