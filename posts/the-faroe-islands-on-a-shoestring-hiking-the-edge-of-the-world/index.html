The Faroe Islands on a Shoestring: Hiking the Edge of the World

2026-07-11 · Destinations · Tags: destinations, backpackerguide, The

There is a place where the grass grows on the roofs, where the wind never truly rests, and where the Atlantic Ocean hammers against cliffs that drop a thousand feet into the sea. The Faroe Islands, an archipelago of 18 volcanic rocks adrift in the North Atlantic, often top the lists of the world's most beautiful destinations. They also top the lists of the world's most expensive. With a reputation for high prices and limited accessibility, this Nordic outpost seems like the antithesis of a budget backpacker's dream. But I'm here to tell you that the dream is not only possible—it's profoundly rewarding. With a bit of cunning, a willingness to trade luxury for adventure, and a pair of sturdy boots, you can traverse this dramatic landscape without draining your savings account. This is your guide to unlocking the raw, untamed beauty of the Faroe Islands on a shoestring budget.

Getting There: The Ferry vs. Flight Gamble

Your first major hurdle is simply getting to this remote archipelago. The two main options are flying or taking the ferry, and each has its own economic calculus. The Smyril Line ferry, the *Norröna*, sails from Hirtshals in Denmark to Tórshavn, the Faroese capital. It’s a scenic, 36-hour journey that can be a budget-friendly option if you book well in advance. A reclining seat in the 'couchette' area can cost as little as €50-70 one way, and you can bring your own food to avoid the pricey onboard restaurant. The ferry also allows you to bring a vehicle, which, while adding to the cost, can be a game-changer for exploring the islands. However, you must factor in the cost of getting to Hirtshals and the time you'll spend at sea.

Flights, on the other hand, are faster but often pricier. Atlantic Airways, the national carrier, flies from Copenhagen, Edinburgh, and a few other European hubs. The key is to book months in advance and to be flexible with your dates. A round-trip flight from Copenhagen can sometimes be found for under €150 if you're lucky and patient. I’ve found that setting price alerts on flight aggregator sites is essential. The real budget hack, however, is to consider a 'stopover' deal. Atlantic Airways sometimes offers deals where you can fly to Iceland or mainland Europe with a free or heavily discounted stopover in the Faroes. This can turn a simple flight into a multi-destination adventure for nearly the same price.

Sleeping for Less: Hostels, Camping, and the 'Law of Jante'

Accommodation is where the Faroe Islands can truly break the bank. Hotels in Tórshavn routinely charge €150-200 per night, which is a non-starter for a budget traveler. But fear not, the backpacker infrastructure is growing. The best option is the **Tórshavn Camping Site**, which is not just for tents. It offers small, basic wooden cabins that sleep two to four people for a fraction of the cost of a hotel. You'll have access to shared kitchen and shower facilities, making it a social hub for budget travelers. A night in a cabin will set you back around €30-40, while pitching your own tent is even cheaper, around €15.

Beyond the capital, the concept of 'allemansrätten' (the right to roam) is not as formalized as in Scandinavia, but wild camping is generally tolerated in the highlands and away from private property. The 'Law of Jante'—a cultural norm that discourages showing off and emphasizes community respect—works in your favor here. If you camp discreetly, leave no trace, and are respectful of the land and livestock, you'll find that locals are incredibly welcoming. I recommend using the official tourist information centers to ask about safe and legal wild camping spots. They are often happy to point you to a flat, grassy area near a village where you can pitch your tent for free, provided you ask permission from the local farmer first. A simple, polite request is almost always met with a smile and a nod.

Navigating the Narrows: Public Transport and the Art of Hitchhiking

Renting a car in the Faroe Islands is exorbitant, often costing €80-100 per day. For a budget traveler, this is simply not an option. Instead, you must master the public transport system, known as *Strandfaraskip Landsins*. The bus network (Bygdaleiðir) connects most villages, and the ferries (Sjóvarleiðir) link the smaller islands. The system is efficient, punctual, and surprisingly affordable. A single ticket can cost anywhere from €2 to €10 depending on the distance, but the real gem is the **Faroe Pass**. This pass gives you unlimited travel on all buses and ferries for a set number of days (typically 4, 7, or 14). It's an absolute bargain and the single best investment you can make for getting around. You can buy it at the main bus terminal in Tórshavn or online.

However, the true budget traveler's secret weapon is hitchhiking. The Faroe Islands have one of the safest and most hitchhiker-friendly cultures in the world. The population is small, and locals are used to seeing travelers on the road. I've had rides from farmers in tractors, fishermen in vans, and even a schoolteacher who went out of her way to drive me to a trailhead. The key is to be patient and smile. Because the distances are short and the roads are winding, you'll rarely wait more than 20 minutes for a ride. Combine hitchhiking with the Faroe Pass for the long hauls, and you have a transportation strategy that costs almost nothing.

Fueling the Adventure: Supermarket Feasts and the Art of the Packed Lunch

Eating out in the Faroe Islands is a luxury you cannot afford. A simple burger in a café will cost you €20, and a sit-down dinner in a restaurant is easily €50 or more. The solution is to embrace the local supermarkets. The main chains are **Bónus** and **Netto**, and they are your best friends. Here, you can find everything you need for a hearty breakfast and a packed lunch. Stock up on oats, bread, cheese, eggs, and local fish. The Faroese are passionate about their food, and you can find high-quality, farm-raised lamb and fresh seafood at reasonable prices if you buy it raw and cook it yourself.

Most hostels and campsites have communal kitchens, so you can cook your own meals. My go-to strategy was to make a large pot of pasta or stew in the evening, which would provide leftovers for the next day's hike. For lunch, a simple sandwich with local cheese and a hard-boiled egg is perfect. Don't forget to pack snacks like nuts, dried fruit, and chocolate for energy on the trail. The only time I splurged was on a single, unforgettable meal of fermented lamb and fresh puffin at a traditional restaurant in Tórshavn—a culinary experience that was worth every króna, but a rare exception to my strict budget. Also, remember that tap water in the Faroe Islands is some of the purest in the world. Fill your water bottle from any stream or tap; it's free, delicious, and perfectly safe.

The Trails That Cost Nothing: Hiking the Postcard Views

The real treasure of the Faroe Islands is not in its museums or restaurants; it's in its dramatic, otherworldly landscape. And the best part? The hiking is completely free. The most famous trail is the hike to **Kallur Lighthouse** on the island of Kalsoy. The path is well-marked and leads you to a cliffside lighthouse with views that are nothing short of cinematic. It's a moderate hike, taking about 2-3 hours round trip, and it's an absolute must-do. Another iconic trek is the **Drangarnir Sea Arch** on Vágar Island. This hike requires a guide due to the tricky terrain and tidal crossings, but it's worth the fee (around €30) for the jaw-dropping views of the arch and the sea stacks. If you're on a strict budget, you can view the arch from the nearby viewpoint at the Miðvágsfoss waterfall, which is also spectacular.

For a more challenging adventure, head to the island of Suðuroy, the southernmost island. The hike to the **Beinisvørð** cliff is a rugged, less-traveled path that rewards you with a sense of absolute solitude and raw power. You'll stand on a 470-meter-high cliff, watching the Atlantic crash below. The key to a successful hike is preparation. The weather can change in an instant, so always carry a waterproof jacket, warm layers, and a map. The official tourist website, visitfaroeislands.com, has excellent resources and downloadable maps. Remember the golden rule: never underestimate the wind. It can be ferocious, especially near the cliffs. Always stay well back from the edge, as the cliffs are unstable and the winds can be strong enough to knock you off your feet.

Conclusion

The Faroe Islands are not a destination you simply visit; they are a place you experience with all your senses. The wind in your hair, the salt on your lips, the sound of a thousand seabirds, and the sight of a landscape that feels like the edge of the world. While the logistics can seem daunting, the islands are a paradise for the resourceful backpacker. By choosing the ferry over the flight, camping over hotels, and supermarket picnics over restaurant meals, you can unlock this Nordic wonderland for a fraction of the cost you'd expect. The Faroe Islands teach you that the most profound adventures are not bought, but earned. They are found in the kindness of a hitchhiker's driver, the warmth of a shared cabin, and the sheer, unadulterated beauty of a trail that costs nothing but your effort. So, pack your boots, embrace the wind, and go chase the edge of the world. Your wallet will thank you, and your soul will be forever changed.
